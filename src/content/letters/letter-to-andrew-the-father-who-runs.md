Dear Andrew,

In my last letters we talked about big questions. Today I want to talk about something that sits underneath them, something that shapes your life more than any idea you'll ever explain to someone else. I want to talk about the picture of God you carry around inside you.

Everyone has one. You may never have put it into words, but it's there. It comes out of its drawer on the day after you've really blown it. The main thing I want you to remember is this: if that picture is colder or less welcoming than the Father Jesus showed us in the story of the prodigal son, you are wrong about God—not Jesus. So let me start with that story, because I think it was meant to correct exactly that picture.

## The father on the road

You know the story in Luke 15. A younger son asks for his share of the inheritance, which in that culture was close to saying, "I wish you were dead." He takes the money, goes far away, and wastes all of it. When he's feeding pigs and wishing he could eat what they eat, he comes to his senses and practices a speech: "I will get up and go to my father, and I will say to him, 'Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you; I am no longer worthy to be called your son; treat me like one of your hired hands'" (Luke 15:18–19).

Notice what he's hoping for. Not a son's place. A job. Probation, really.

Now watch the father. "But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms around him and kissed him" (Luke 15:20).

Three things there, Andrew. First, the father **saw him while he was still far off**. You only see someone far off if you've been watching the road. This father had been looking for him, day after day. Second, he **ran**. In that world a dignified older man did not run. He didn't care how it looked. Third, he embraced and kissed his son **before the speech was finished**. The son gets out the first part, "I am no longer worthy to be called your son," and the father cuts him off. He never gets to the line about the hired hands. The father won't even hear it. Instead: "Quickly, bring out a robe—the best one—and put it on him; put a ring on his finger and sandals on his feet" (Luke 15:22). Robe, ring, sandals. Those are not a servant's things. They're a son's.

And the story doesn't end there. The older brother, the one who stayed home and did everything right, hears the party and gets angry and won't go in. And again the father goes out. "His father came out and began to plead with him" (Luke 15:28). He pleads. He says, "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours" (Luke 15:31). The father runs toward the son who went wild, and he walks out to the son who stayed home and stayed cold. He goes after both of them.

That is Jesus telling us what God is like.

Here is the heart of this letter:

> If your picture of God is colder, less compassionate, or less willing to welcome you than the Father Jesus shows us in the prodigal son, you are wrong about God—not Jesus. Jesus isn’t the soft side of a harder God. He shows us who the Father really is.

## The picture that shows up when you fail

So here's the test. Each of us carries a picture of God somewhere inside, and it shows up when we fail. Some imagine a God who waits to see if you've suffered enough. Others imagine one who chose before you were born whether to love you, or one who takes you back but keeps you on probation. Hold each of those up to that running father. "Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9). That welcome is not indifference to what the son did. The father ran because he loved him, not because the waste didn't matter.

It's easy to agree with that and hard to believe it all the way down. And we live out of the picture we carry. Let me show you three places.

**How you pray.** If deep down you think God is irritated with you, you'll pray like someone filling out a form at the principal's office, careful and brief, hoping to get out without trouble. But if God is the father watching the road, prayer is just turning toward someone who is already turned toward you. You can bring him the small things and the embarrassing things. Jesus said, "Is there anyone among you who, if your child asks for bread, will give a stone? Or if the child asks for a fish, will give a snake? If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him!" (Matthew 7:9–11). Notice that "how much more."

**How you treat people.** We tend to become like the God we worship. People who picture a God keeping score usually keep score too. That was the older brother's problem. He had worked for his father for years, but he thought of it as slaving for a boss: "For all these years I have been working like a slave for you" (Luke 15:29). His picture of his father was wrong, and so he couldn't stand to see his brother welcomed. If you picture a running God, you'll find yourself running toward people too, even the person you disagree with.

**How you come back after failing.** This is the big one. When you mess up, and you will, because everyone does, what do you do next? If your picture is the God who waits until you've suffered enough, you'll punish yourself for a while first, and only then creep back. If your picture is the probation God, you'll come back but stay at arm's length, trying to earn your way back into good standing. Neither is how the son came home. He came home ashamed, with a speech ready, and he was met on the road. You don't need to suffer first. You don't need to earn your robe back. You only need to turn around and start walking. You will find he's already been running toward you.

I've warned about what I called "gospels of sin management," where the whole thing becomes getting our sins handled, with little room for actually living with God. A sin-management picture of God is always some kind of accountant. The running father is not an accountant. He's a father.

## Jesus is the exact picture

How do we know which picture is right? We look at Jesus. This is the whole key, and the New Testament says it over and over.

"No one has ever seen God. It is God the only Son, who is close to the Father's heart, who has made him known" (John 1:18).

"He is the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15).

"He is the reflection of God's glory and the exact imprint of God's very being" (Hebrews 1:3).

The exact imprint. Like a seal pressed into warm wax. That means there's no hidden God behind Jesus who is different from Jesus. There's no harder face behind the kind one. When Philip said, "Show us the Father," Jesus answered, "Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know me? Whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9).

So let's look at what we see when we look at Jesus.

**We see God weeping over people who reject him.** As Jesus came near Jerusalem, the city that would soon demand his death, "he wept over it, saying, 'If you, even you, had only recognized on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes'" (Luke 19:41–42). And at another time: "Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!" (Matthew 23:37). Andrew, look at that. When people refuse God, he doesn't shrug, and he doesn't gloat. He weeps. He wants to gather them like a mother hen. Whatever the Bible means about judgment, and it means something serious, it never means God is glad to lose anyone. The prophet Hosea heard God say the same thing about Israel centuries earlier: "How can I give you up, Ephraim? How can I hand you over, O Israel? ... My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender" (Hosea 11:8).

**We see God weeping with people who grieve.** When Jesus came to the tomb of his friend Lazarus, the shortest verse in the Bible says it all: "Jesus began to weep" (John 11:35). He knew he was about to raise Lazarus, and he wept anyway. That's what God is like with you when you're hurting.

**We see God going after the lost.** Right before the running father, Jesus told two other stories in the same chapter. A shepherd with a hundred sheep loses one and goes "after the one that is lost until he finds it." Then "he lays it on his shoulders and rejoices" (Luke 15:4–5). A woman loses one silver coin, and she will "light a lamp, sweep the house, and search carefully until she finds it" (Luke 15:8). Notice who does the searching. God does, and when he finds you, he isn't annoyed at the trouble. He throws a party.

**We see God welcoming people everyone else had written off.** Zacchaeus was a tax collector who had cheated his own people. The crowd hated him. Jesus stopped under his tree and said, "Zacchaeus, hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today" (Luke 19:5). He didn't make Zacchaeus clean up first. The welcome came first, and the change came after it. Then Jesus said, "For the Son of Man came to seek out and to save the lost" (Luke 19:10).

**We see God refusing to condemn, and calling to something better.** When a woman caught in adultery was dragged in front of him, and the men with stones had all walked away, Jesus said, "Woman, where are they? Has no one condemned you?" She said, "No one, sir." And Jesus said, "Neither do I condemn you. Go your way, and from now on do not sin again" (John 8:10–11). Look at the order: no condemnation first, then a new life. That's not a soft God. It's a love strong enough to change you.

**We see God forgiving even while he is being killed.** On the cross, with nails in his hands, Jesus prayed, "Father, forgive them; for they do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). If you ever wonder whether there's some failure big enough to put you outside God's love, remember that he prayed for the people crucifying him.

Paul put it simply: "But God proves his love for us in that while we still were sinners Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). Not after. While. And John says it in the plainest words in the Bible: "God is love" (1 John 4:8). Then he adds, "In this is love, not that we loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son" (1 John 4:10). God's love doesn't respond to our good behavior. It comes first.

This wasn't a new idea that showed up with Jesus, either. The psalmist saw it long before: "The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love... As a father has compassion for his children, so the Lord has compassion for those who fear him" (Psalm 103:8, 13). Jesus didn't invent a kinder God. He showed us, in a human face, the God who had always been there.

## How to test and correct your picture

Now, how do you actually fix a wrong picture of God? You can't just decide to feel differently. Pictures don't change by willpower. They change by looking at the true thing long enough that it replaces the false one. So here's a simple practice. I'd like you to try it for a month.

**1. Catch the picture in the act.** The next time you fail, notice what you expect God to feel about you. Don't argue with it yet. Just notice. Is he frowning? Disappointed? Waiting with his arms crossed? Bored with you? Write down a word or two if that helps. You're just bringing the hidden picture into the light.

**2. Hold it up to Jesus.** Then ask one honest question: "Is this how Jesus treated people like me?" Go back to the stories. The son on the road. Zacchaeus in the tree. The woman with no one left to condemn her. If your picture is colder, slower, or more grudging than Jesus, then you know which one to throw out. Not Jesus.

**3. Read one Gospel story a day, slowly.** Pick a story from Matthew, Mark, Luke, or John. Read it twice. As you read, ask, "What does this show me about what God is like?" Over a month you'll have looked at thirty pictures of the real God. That does more than any argument can.

**4. Come back fast.** When you fail, practice coming home quickly. Don't wait until you've felt bad enough. Just turn and say, "Father, I've done wrong. Thank you that you're already running toward me. Help me to do better." Then get up and go on with him. This isn't cheap grace. As I once wrote, "Grace is not opposed to effort, it is opposed to earning." You'll still work hard to live well. But you'll work as a son at home, not as a hired hand trying to earn his keep.

You'll find, over time, that the fear begins to drain out. John says, "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear; for fear has to do with punishment" (1 John 4:18). Fear is a sign that your picture still has a punisher in it. As you look at Jesus, love will slowly push the fear out. Not all at once, but surely.

## A last word

Andrew, here is something I've learned over a long life. Clear thinking matters. But the picture of God you live with matters more. If your picture of God is colder, less compassionate, or less willing to welcome you than the Father Jesus shows us in the prodigal son, you are wrong about God—not Jesus. Jesus isn’t the soft side of a harder God. He shows us who the Father really is. Live with the running father instead, and you'll be free to pray honestly, to love people generously, and to come home quickly every time you wander. And you'll find that life with God isn't something you wait for until after you die. It starts now, on the road, in his arms.

So the next time you've blown it, and you stand there practicing your speech about how you're not worthy, look up. Look down the road. You'll see someone running toward you, robe flying, arms already open. He's been watching for you the whole time. He always has been.

With love, and with great joy in you,

Dallas
